The integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools into the undergraduate writing landscape presents a complex pedagogical challenge, demanding a nuanced understanding of how these technologies interact with the core cognitive processes of critical thought. The central question is whether AI improves or undermines the development of critical thinking skills. To answer this, it is crucial to distinguish between the outright *offloading of cognition* and the strategic use of AI for *supported drafting*. When AI is used as a cognitive crutch—a method of offloading the demanding labor of synthesis, argumentation, or initial drafting—it risks undermining the development of critical thinking. This process bypasses the necessary struggle with ambiguity and structure that underpins deep learning. Instead of grappling with a difficult thesis statement or organizing disparate ideas, the student relies on the AI to generate a polished, coherent output, thereby sidestepping the critical act of intellectual assembly. The resulting work may appear sophisticated, but it lacks the authentic intellectual residue of the student’s own critical engagement.

Conversely, when AI is employed for supported drafting, its utility shifts from being a replacement for thought to being an advanced scaffolding tool. In this capacity, AI can function as a sophisticated brainstorming partner, an outlining aid, or a source of immediate feedback on clarity and tone, allowing the student to focus their cognitive energy on higher-order tasks. For example, a student can prompt an AI to generate three opposing viewpoints on a historical event, and then use the AI’s output not as the final product, but as a structured dataset for comparison and critique. This process forces the student to engage in meta-cognition—thinking about how to think—and requires them to critically evaluate the AI’s premises, biases, and completeness. This practice mirrors the scholarly process of engaging with diverse primary sources, but with the added benefit of immediate, low-stakes iteration.

The danger lies in the pedagogical misinterpretation of AI assistance. If instructors grade solely on the final product without assessing the process of critical refinement, the incentive structure encourages offloading. A study by Baker et al. (2023) suggests that while AI can improve fluency and grammatical accuracy, the reliance on these tools without intervention may decrease the depth of critical textual analysis, particularly in tasks requiring original conceptual mapping. Therefore, the improvement of critical thinking is not inherent to the technology itself, but rather contingent upon the pedagogical framework surrounding its use.

Ultimately, generative AI does not inherently improve or undermine critical thinking; rather, it acts as a powerful amplifier of existing habits. It undermines critical thinking when it facilitates intellectual laziness, allowing students to outsource the difficult work of synthesis. It improves critical thinking, however, when it is purposefully integrated into a scaffolded learning model that demands the student use the AI's output as raw material—material that must be interrogated, challenged, and critically re-authored. The role of the undergraduate writer must therefore evolve from being a mere producer of text to being a skilled editor and critical curator of information, mastering the art of prompting and critically refining AI-generated drafts.
